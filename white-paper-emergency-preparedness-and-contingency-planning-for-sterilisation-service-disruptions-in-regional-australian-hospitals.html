Executive Summary

Surgery, obstetrics, emergency procedures and many outpatient procedures depend on a continuous supply of sterile reusable medical devices (RMDs). Loss of reprocessing capacity stops these services once reserve sterile stock is consumed. Regional, rural and remote hospitals carry greater exposure than metropolitan hospitals because they operate fewer reprocessing units, rely on small specialist teams, and face longer response times for service technicians, spare parts, freight and inter-facility transport.

This paper sets out contingency measures for public and private regional hospitals covering equipment redundancy, utility resilience, pre-qualified alternative processing routes, workforce cover, inter-facility support and incident reporting. AS 5369:2023 requires a business continuity plan with documented backup procedures, and the process to activate them, so that reprocessing requirements are met under emergency and suboptimal operating conditions.

Key Points

  • Failure Modes: Equipment faults, loss of power, steam, water or air conditioning, process contamination, workforce absence, supply interruption and site isolation each stop instrument reprocessing. In 2019 a single process fault stopped elective surgery at Austin Hospital, Melbourne, and at Princess Alexandra Hospital, Brisbane.

  • Redundancy: Provide two washer-disinfectors and two steam sterilisers, the configuration the Australasian Health Facility Guidelines (AusHFG) give as preferred for smaller, rural and remote sterilising services units (SSUs). Place at least one washer-disinfector, one steriliser and the sterile store on emergency power.

  • Alternative Processing: Pre-qualify every alternative route (second steriliser, small steam steriliser, partner facility, contracted reprocessor) for the loads it will carry. Low-temperature processes, high-level disinfection and immediate-use sterilisation do not replace lost steam sterilisation capacity.

  • Workforce: Train and assess staff outside the SSU as competent for defined tasks, and pre-arrange relief staff through the health service, hospital group or agencies.

  • Cost: At the regional base case of the cost model, a partner SSU agreement returns about 17 times its annual cost. Duplicating the washer-disinfector and steriliser breaks even at an installed cost of about $287,800 for the two added units, rising to about $801,600 when each unit is sized for the full peak daily load.

  • Reporting and Compliance: Notify through the jurisdiction’s incident management system within its timeframes. AS 5369:2023 supersedes AS/NZS 4187:2014, and ACSQHC Advisory AS24/01 applies it to NSQHS Standards Action 3.17.

Introduction

Sterilising services clean, disinfect, inspect, assemble, package, sterilise and store RMDs, and record the traceability of each critical RMD to an individual steriliser cycle. A disruption at any stage reduces the supply of sterile instrument sets. In a hospital with one washer-disinfector or one steriliser, a single fault removes all local capacity, and AusHFG notes that distance limits access to technicians and repairs for rural and remote services.

This paper covers public and private hospitals in regional, rural and remote Australia. It addresses the SSU, its utilities and the arrangements that maintain sterile supply when the SSU is unavailable. AS 5369:2023, published 15 December 2023, supersedes AS/NZS 4187:2014 and AS/NZS 4815:2006 and is the reference standard throughout.

Risk Overview: Causes and Impacts of Sterilisation Service Disruptions

Equipment Failure: Washer-disinfectors, sterilisers, drying cabinets and ultrasonic cleaners fail through wear of door seals, gaskets, pumps, valves, heating elements, sensors and controls. A fault that creates a quality defect can remove more capacity than the failed unit. At Austin Hospital, Melbourne, in September 2019, a rubber seal disintegrated in a surgical instrument washer and deposited rubber particles on washed instruments. All washers were withdrawn from service after the fault was identified, and more than 500 elective procedures were cancelled. The Victorian Government directed that the highest priority patients be treated in private hospitals at government cost. The washers involved were replaced.

Utility Loss: Steam sterilisers require electricity, steam from a building boiler or a dedicated steam generator, treated feedwater, compressed air where pneumatic door seals or valves are fitted, seal and cooling water where liquid ring vacuum pumps are fitted, and drainage. Washer-disinfectors require electricity, hot and cold water, treated final rinse water and drainage. Loss of any one of these stops the process.

Water Quality: A boil-water notice, flood or main break changes the quality of water entering the SSU. AS 5369:2023 limits final rinse water for washer-disinfectors processing surgical instruments to pH 5.5 to 8.0, conductivity not greater than 30 µS/cm at 20 deg C [68 deg F], total viable count not greater than 100 cfu/100 mL and endotoxin not greater than 0.25 EU/mL. Feedwater to a dedicated steam generator is limited to conductivity not greater than 5 µS/cm at 20 deg C [68 deg F]. Where water is used untreated, the facility risk assessment should define the response to significant excursions in supply quality.

Steam Supply and Process Contamination: A process can run within its cycle parameters and still produce unusable output. At Princess Alexandra Hospital, Brisbane, in August 2019, a change to the chemical dosed into the steam boilers left a residue on the wrapping of sterilised packs. The instruments were confirmed sterile, but the packs were withdrawn, at least 46 procedures were postponed within 24 hours, and other Metro South facilities and the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital processed instruments while the steam lines were flushed. AS 5369:2023 requires OQ when a service is changed and steam quality testing when the steam supply changes, with acceptance criteria of dryness value not less than 0.95, non-condensable gases not greater than 3.5 % V/V and superheat not greater than 25 deg C [45 deg F]. Where sterilisers draw on a non-dedicated steam supply, steam condensate purity is tested at IQ or OQ and annually thereafter. Treat any change to boiler water treatment as a change to the steam supply.

Environmental Control Failure: Loss of ventilation or air conditioning allows sterile store temperature and humidity to drift outside storage limits. RMDs with moisture on or in the packaging are treated as contaminated and must be reprocessed, which reduces available stock.

Workforce Loss: Small regional SSUs may be staffed by one or two technicians. Illness, leave, resignation or an outbreak stops the service if no other staff are trained and assessed as competent. AS 5369:2023 requires staffing sufficient for continuous, safe operation, a formal orientation and training program, and periodic competency assessment at intervals defined by the facility.

Consumables and Loan Sets: Reprocessing depends on packaging materials, chemical and biological indicators, air removal test packs, detergents and water treatment consumables, which remote sites receive on infrequent freight schedules. AusHFG notes that rural and remote facilities may hold loan sets and transport cases longer to suit courier timeframes, which requires additional storage space. AS 5369:2023 requires validated reprocessing instructions for loan sets and processing by a validated cleaning and further processing method on receipt.

Natural Disasters and Isolation: Floods, bushfires and cyclones can damage equipment, cut utilities for extended periods and close transport routes while increasing demand for emergency procedures. Set reserve sterile stock, single-use stock and consumable holdings against the longest credible isolation period for the site.

Service Impact: Loss of reprocessing capacity first affects surgery, obstetrics, emergency procedures and endoscopy. The response is to continue emergency and time-critical procedures on reserve stock, defer elective cases and transfer patients where capacity cannot be restored in time. Any possibility that an inadequately reprocessed RMD reached a patient requires recall of affected items, incident reporting, infection prevention and control follow-up and open disclosure.

Contingency Planning Framework

Document each contingency measure in the hospital business continuity plan with activation triggers, responsible roles and a procedure for each failure scenario. AS 5369:2023 requires reprocessing policies and procedures to include business continuity and contingency planning for emergencies, and requires a contingency plan for malfunction or breakdown of high-level disinfection equipment used for heat-labile semi-critical RMDs. Exercise the plan at defined intervals, including a scenario with total loss of the SSU.

1. Backup Systems and Redundant Equipment

Equipment Redundancy: AusHFG HPU 0190 (Revision 7.0, 2022) gives two washer-disinfectors, two automated flexible endoscope reprocessors and two steam sterilisers as the preferred configuration for smaller, rural and remote SSUs, to provide redundancy for maintenance and breakdown. AS 5369:2023 guidance calls for redundancy covering maintenance, calibration, qualification, requalification and unscheduled downtime. Size each unit of a duplicated pair to process at least the minimum essential load alone (emergency surgery, obstetric and emergency department sets), and preferably the full peak daily load within extended hours. Where a single unit is retained, document the alternative route and the delivery time for sterile sets from it.

Low-Temperature Processes: AS 5369:2023 requires critical RMDs that tolerate moist heat to be terminally sterilised by a validated moist heat process. Low-temperature processes are for RMDs that cannot withstand moist heat and whose instructions for use (IFU) specify that process. A low-temperature steriliser therefore provides no backup capacity for steam-compatible instrument sets.

Preventive Maintenance and Return to Service: Perform preventive maintenance to the equipment IFU, with no more than 12 months between scheduled maintenance on infrequently used equipment. Trend air removal test failures, leak rate results, aborted cycles, extended cycle times and drying failures, and act on adverse trends before failure. Hold critical spares on site, including door seals, gaskets, solenoid valves and temperature and pressure sensors. AS 5369:2023 requires OQ performance tests after repair and before return to service, and requalification after major repairs that could affect process efficacy. Agree in advance with the service provider which tests apply to each repair type and where the test instrumentation will come from, so that return to service is not held for test equipment or personnel.

Emergency Power: AusHFG recommends that at least one washer-disinfector, one steriliser and the sterile store are on emergency power supply. Confirm that the generator has spare capacity for the steriliser and any electric steam generator, vacuum pump, water treatment plant, compressed air plant and sterile store air handling. Where sterilisers draw on building steam, place the boiler controls, feed pumps and water treatment on the essential supply. Where building steam is unreliable, specify sterilisers with an integral electric steam generator as a second steam source. After any power or steam interruption, complete the leak rate test and the air removal and steam penetration test before releasing loads.

Water Treatment: Treated water is a common point of failure for washer-disinfectors and dedicated steam generators. Provide duplex reverse osmosis (RO) trains or treated water storage sized for a defined period of operation. For small steam sterilisers, hold a reserve of purified water that meets the steriliser feedwater specification. After any mains water event, test treated water against the final rinse and feedwater limits before resuming processing.

Sterile Store Environment: AS 5369:2023 recommends sterile storage at 18 to 25 deg C [64 to 77 deg F] and 35 to 70 % relative humidity (RH), and notes that emergency power may be required for ventilation of sterile stores serving operating theatres. Monitor temperature and RH continuously through the building management system or data loggers, with alarms set inside these limits so that corrective action starts before an excursion. NSW Clinical Excellence Commission guidance (2024) sets a risk assessment trigger at 8 hours outside range, escalation within 2 to 8 hours of onset, and reprocessing of RMDs with moisture on or in the packaging. Nominate a climate-controlled relocation area for sterile stock.

Single-Use Sterile Stock: Hold single-use sterile kits for defined emergency procedures, for example suturing, chest drain insertion, obstetric delivery and minor surgical procedures. Size the holding to the longest credible isolation period or resupply interval, and rotate by expiry date. AS 5369:2023 prohibits reprocessing or reuse of used single-use devices, and permits reprocessing of opened but unused single-use devices only where their reprocessing instructions allow it.

Temporary Equipment: Hired or containerised reprocessing units restore capacity where on-site repair will take longer than delivery and installation. Confirm in advance the supplier, lead time, transport access, hardstand or floor loading, and connection requirements (electrical supply rating, water, treated water, drainage and steam). Consider providing a permanent external connection point. AS 5369:2023 requires OQ immediately after installation or relocation, followed by PQ for the loads to be processed.

2. Staffing Resilience and Emergency Workforce Plans

Cross-Training: Train perioperative and other clinical staff in defined SSU tasks such as set assembly, packaging, steriliser loading, cycle selection and routine tests. Assess and record competency for each task, reassess at defined intervals, and restrict load release to staff assessed as competent for that function.

Relief Staffing: Arrange relief from larger facilities in the same local health district, hospital and health service or private hospital group, and from agencies. Pre-arrange credentialing, travel, accommodation and orientation to local equipment and procedures. Keep a current contact list in the business continuity plan.

Succession: Maintain at least two staff competent in each critical SSU function. Support staff to complete the nationally recognised Certificate III in Sterilisation Services, and record equipment fault histories and local operating knowledge in controlled procedures.

Task Reallocation: Define which routine work stops on activation, for example ward and outpatient reprocessing, so that SSU staff process emergency sets first. Assign the additional duties that perioperative staff take on.

Fatigue Management: Backlog recovery drives extended shifts. Set maximum shift lengths and rest breaks, use relief or cross-trained staff to hold them, and apply an independent check of set assembly and load release during recovery.

Incident Leadership: Name the SSU manager and an alternate, such as the perioperative nurse unit manager or the infection prevention and control lead, as incident lead for reprocessing failures.

3. Cross-Facility Collaboration and Resource Sharing

Inter-facility support is effective only when agreed and tested before an event.

Public Hospital Networks: Designate a partner SSU, normally the regional base hospital, for each smaller hospital. Record in a memorandum of understanding the processing services, sterile set loans, transport arrangements, traceability, communication contacts and cost recovery. Confirm that the partner’s validated processes suit the sending hospital’s RMDs, including cycle compatibility, set mass and packaging, and that instrument tracking records can be exchanged.

Public and Private Shared Services: A permanent shared service is a valid design option. In 2018 Murrumbidgee Local Health District contracted St Vincent’s Private Community Hospital Griffith to provide sterilising services to Griffith Base Hospital, sharing a single sterilising unit, with instruments moved by a hospital transport vehicle. For emergency support, identify the nearest public or private SSU with spare capacity and agree activation contacts in advance.

Contracted Reprocessors: AS 5369:2023 states that an agreement should be in place for outsourced reprocessing, defining each party’s responsibilities, including conformance with the standard. Its guidance calls for the contract to include evidence of accreditation, audit or equivalent quality activity. Put the contract and transport arrangements in place before an event, even where routine use is not planned.

Shared Sterile Stock: A partner facility can lend standard sterile sets, for example caesarean section or laparotomy sets, to cover a short outage. Record each loan in both tracking systems so that every item remains traceable to its sterilisation cycle.

Patient Transfer: Where sterile RMDs cannot be supplied in time, transfer the patient to a facility with sterile supply. Do not use RMDs of uncertain reprocessing status.

Transport: AS 5369:2023 requires a documented procedure for transporting used RMDs, and a risk assessment with documented nonconformance conditions for transport of reprocessed RMDs between sites. Use rigid, robust, leak-proof containers that can be sealed, labelled and cleaned. Specify the maximum time from use to reprocessing and the point-of-use pre-treatment that prevents soil drying in transit. Evaluate and document revalidation requirements whenever a new transport route or means is introduced. For remote sites, align processing with road and air freight schedules and fund courier costs in the contingency budget.

4. Communication, Reporting and Regulatory Compliance

Incident Management: On confirmation of a reprocessing failure, activate the hospital incident management structure with executive, perioperative, SSU, infection prevention and control, engineering and communications representation. Issue status updates at fixed intervals stating available sterile stock, procedures affected, expected restoration time and changes to the operating list.

Notification: Report through the jurisdiction’s incident management system. In NSW public health services, PD2020_047 requires the Chief Executive to contact the Ministry of Health immediately for clinical incidents with the potential to become a matter of public interest. A Reportable Incident Brief (Part A within 24 hours, Part B within 72 hours) is required for Harm Score 1 incidents and for other incidents the Chief Executive determines, including clinical incidents arising from serious systemic problems. NSW licensed private health facilities report reportable incidents to the Ministry’s Regulation and Compliance Unit within 2 working days. Name the role responsible for each notification in the business continuity plan.

Recall and Traceability: AS 5369:2023 requires timely recall of released RMDs where there is evidence of failure during cleaning, disinfection, packaging, sterilisation, transport or storage, and traceability of each critical RMD to the individual steriliser cycle. Use cycle records to define the affected population and reconcile recalled quantities.

Device and Process Issues: Where a device malfunction or process chemical caused the disruption, report it to the TGA and the product sponsor, quarantine the affected components, and retain cycle records, test results and photographs for root cause analysis. Monitor TGA recall notices through the System for Australian Recall Actions (SARA).

Open Disclosure: Where a patient may have been exposed to an inadequately reprocessed RMD, apply the Australian Open Disclosure Framework. For deferred procedures, inform patients of the cause and the rescheduling plan.

Standards Compliance: ACSQHC Advisory AS24/01 (November 2024) applies AS 5369:2023 to NSQHS Standards Action 3.17, required a gap analysis by 30 June 2025, and requires a compliance plan with timeframes. Contingency processes must still meet AS 5369:2023 and the Australian Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Infection in Healthcare (2019). Alternative processes require the same validation, routine monitoring and records as primary processes. Retain the business continuity plan and exercise records as accreditation evidence.

Post-Incident Review: Review cause, response timeline, stock consumption and decision points with SSU, perioperative, infection prevention and control, engineering and executive staff within the timeframe set by the incident policy. Update the business continuity plan, and use downtime and cancellation data to support capital requests for redundancy.

5. Cost Model: Outage Cost and Contingency Investment

Purpose: The model estimates the expected annual cost of reprocessing outages at a regional hospital and the installed cost at which each contingency measure breaks even. It uses public Australian cost data and stated planning assumptions. No equipment prices are used. Each measure is expressed as a break-even installed cost for comparison against site quotations. Costs are in Australian dollars in real terms. Index all inputs to the appraisal year and replace assumptions with site data before use.

Downtime Method: Unit unavailability is the number of unplanned failures per year multiplied by the mean time to restore in days, divided by 365. A site with one washer-disinfector and one steriliser in series is fully unavailable when either unit is down. A duplicated site is fully unavailable only when both units of the same type are down together, which for independent failures occurs with a probability equal to the square of unit unavailability. With one unit of a type down, the duplicated site runs at reduced capacity. Common-mode events (loss of power, steam or water quality) stop every unit regardless of configuration. Planned downtime for preventive maintenance and annual requalification is added separately. In a single-unit site it stops the service. In a duplicated site it reduces capacity.

Cost Method: Cost per outage day is the cost of cancelled elective procedures plus off-site processing, transport and overtime. An unplanned cancellation incurs stranded cost (theatre staffing and sessional costs already committed) plus a recovery premium (additional sessions or purchase from another provider). A planned cancellation, rostered in advance, incurs the recovery premium only. Two responses are modelled for a full outage: cancel all elective procedures, or process sets at a partner SSU 100 km [62 miles] away so that half of the elective procedures proceed. Emergency, obstetric and emergency department sets are processed off-site every outage day in both responses. Capital break-even is the avoided annual cost, less added operating cost, multiplied by the annuity factor. The annuity factor at 7 % real is 7.024 for 10 years and 4.100 for 5 years (3 %: 8.530 and 4.580, 10 %: 6.145 and 3.791). Excluded costs, including clinical harm from delay, patient travel, recall and infection follow-up, would increase the outage cost.

Implications for Design: The partner SSU agreement and cross-training return the highest value for the lowest cost at every location. Implement them first. At the regional base case, upsizing each unit from 80 % to 100 % of peak load avoids more cost ($73,100 per year) than duplication at 80 % capacity ($66,000 per year). Specify each unit of a duplicated pair to carry the full peak daily load within extended hours. Second steam sources and SSU emergency power are justified by outage cost only where site event history shows frequent or long utility failures, so base those decisions on site event records. Small steam sterilisers are justified mainly at remote sites and for isolation scenarios.

Applying the Model: Derive failures per unit per year and time to restore from at least 3 years of work orders, including parts and technician travel time. Take procedures per list day and sets per procedure from the theatre management and instrument tracking systems. Replace the national average episode cost with patient-level costing for the affected case mix. Obtain partner processing charges and transport costs in writing. Compare the resulting break-even installed costs with site quotations that include building services, installation, IQ, OQ and PQ, and repeat the appraisal at 3 % and 10 %.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Apply the following measures in every regional hospital that performs procedures requiring sterile RMDs.

  • Contingency Plan: Write a sterilisation contingency procedure within the business continuity plan covering equipment failure, utility loss, water and steam contamination, environmental control failure, workforce loss and site isolation. Define activation triggers, responsible roles and the activation process, and exercise the procedure at defined intervals.

  • Redundancy: Provide two washer-disinfectors and two steam sterilisers, each sized for at least the minimum essential load and preferably the full peak daily load within extended hours. Where this cannot be justified, provide a small steam steriliser with a Type B cycle qualified for the emergency sets and document the off-site route.

  • Utilities: Place at least one washer-disinfector, one steriliser, the sterile store and their supporting plant (steam generation, water treatment, compressed air) on emergency power. Provide duplex water treatment or treated water storage.

  • Steam and Water Change Control: Treat any change to boiler water treatment or water supply as a service change, and complete steam quality, condensate purity or water quality testing before releasing loads.

  • Maintenance: Hold critical spares, trend routine test results, and agree return-to-service test requirements for each repair type with the service provider.

  • Reserve Stock: Hold reserve sterile sets and single-use kits sized to the longest credible isolation period, and rotate by expiry date.

  • Mutual Aid: Execute written agreements with a partner SSU or contracted reprocessor covering processing, set loans, transport, traceability and cost recovery, and test them with periodic small-scale transfers.

  • Workforce: Maintain at least two competent staff for each critical SSU function, cross-train perioperative staff for defined tasks, and pre-arrange relief staffing.

  • Restricted Processes: Do not use low-temperature processes, high-level disinfection or immediate-use sterilisation to replace lost steam sterilisation capacity. Do not reprocess or reuse used single-use devices.

  • Cost Appraisal: Calculate expected annual outage cost from site failure history, time to restore, elective volume and patient-level case cost. Express each contingency measure as a break-even installed cost at 7 % real over its effective life, and compare it with site quotations.

  • Reporting: Specify notification roles and timeframes for the jurisdiction, the recall procedure and the open disclosure process in the business continuity plan.
