Comparative Environmental Footprint of Single-Use vs Reusable Instrument Processing in Australia

Introduction

Australian health care accounted for approximately 7% of national greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the 2014 to 2015 financial year, with public and private hospitals contributing approximately 44% of the sector total. Operating theatres generate approximately 20 to 30% of hospital waste. Instrument selection therefore has a measurable effect on facility waste, energy, water and GHG performance.

This paper compares single-use and reusable surgical and dental instrument processing in Australia across four impact categories: solid waste, energy, water and GHG emissions. It concentrates on the use and disposal phases, which the facility controls directly, and sets out the operating conditions under which each approach performs best in large hospitals and in small and regional facilities.

Background: Single-Use and Reusable Instruments in Surgical and Dental Practice

Single-use instruments are supplied sterile, used on one patient and discarded. They are typically manufactured from polymers or lower-grade stainless steel. Examples include scalpels, some forceps and scissors, saliva ejectors, plastic mouth mirrors and some dental burs and endodontic files. They remove reprocessing from the point of care, which suits facilities without validated sterilisation capacity, outreach services and low-volume procedures. Each use generates contaminated waste and a repeat purchase.

Reusable instruments are manufactured from surgical-grade stainless steel or titanium to withstand repeated cleaning, disinfection and sterilisation. Examples include forceps, clamps, retractors, scissors, dental elevators, explorers and scalers. Each use requires cleaning, inspection, packaging, sterilisation, storage and traceability. This in turn requires washer-disinfectors, ultrasonic cleaners, steam sterilisers, drying equipment, a compliant reprocessing area and competent staff. Manufacturing impact and purchase cost are amortised across the service life of the instrument, and many complex surgical instruments are available only in reusable form.

Recent Trends and Changes in Practice

Reusable stainless steel instruments were standard in surgery and dentistry until single-use alternatives expanded in the late twentieth century, driven by infection control requirements and convenience. Single-use options now exist for most categories of device traditionally reprocessed in general practice.

Reprocessing requirements have also tightened. Following the transition to AS/NZS 4187:2014, one Queensland public oral health service removed benchtop sterilisers from its clinics and centralised reprocessing, with a daily courier exchanging instruments between 28 satellite facilities and a central sterilisation department. In December 2023, AS 5369:2023 superseded AS/NZS 4187:2014 and AS/NZS 4815:2006. It applies one set of requirements to hospitals and to office-based practices including medical, dental and podiatry clinics. A facility that cannot meet these requirements in-house must upgrade its reprocessing capacity, use a compliant central service, or substitute single-use devices. Each option changes the facility's environmental footprint.

Regulatory and Policy Context in Australia

AS 5369:2023 prohibits the reprocessing or reuse of a used device labelled or intended for single use. It classifies reprocessing of single-use devices as a manufacturing activity regulated by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). Facilities therefore choose between single-use devices and reusable devices supplied with validated reprocessing instructions.

National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards Action 3.17 requires health service organisations to reprocess reusable medical devices in line with current national standards, and is assessed at accreditation. The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care required organisations using critical and semi-critical devices to complete a gap analysis against AS 5369:2023 by 30 June 2025 and to hold a compliance plan with defined timeframes. No fixed compliance deadline applies.

AS 5369:2023 requires a quality management system, staff competency, validated cleaning and sterilisation processes, traceability of critical devices to the sterilisation cycle, and a unidirectional workflow from dirty to clean activities. A device that cannot be cleaned to the specified requirement must not be reprocessed, and a sterile single-use alternative should be considered. Before purchasing a reusable device, confirm it can be reprocessed with existing equipment and staff competency, or budget for additional qualified equipment. Where a steriliser in a remote office-based practice cannot be calibrated and validated on site, AS 5369:2023 permits off-site validation followed by performance checks on return.

Clinical waste is regulated by each state and territory and must be incinerated or treated before landfill disposal. Clinical waste disposal costs more per kilogram [per pound] than general waste disposal. Segregate uncontaminated packaging from clinical waste to reduce both disposal cost and treatment emissions. Stainless steel instruments retired from service can be recycled as scrap metal after decontamination.

Environmental Impact Comparison: Single-Use vs Reusable Instruments

Compare options on a life cycle basis covering manufacture, transport, use including reprocessing, and end-of-life treatment. Express results per use or per procedure, not per item purchased. Use and disposal are the phases a facility controls directly and are the focus of this section. The findings below draw on a systematic review of 27 healthcare product life cycle assessments (LCAs) and on device-level studies.

Solid Waste Generation

Every single-use instrument becomes clinical waste after one procedure, together with its sterile barrier packaging. These items are contaminated after use and are rarely recovered. Reusable instruments generate reprocessing consumables, such as sterilisation wrap, pouches and chemical indicators, and become waste only at end of service life, when stainless steel retains scrap value.

In the systematic review, studies of non-invasive reusable devices reported solid waste reductions of up to 99% compared with single-use equivalents. A cost-factor analysis of a Queensland public oral health service, comprising one main clinic and 28 satellite facilities, modelled annual waste at 4,559 kg [10,050 lb] for its current mixed practice, 47 kg [104 lb] for all-reusable instruments and 12,590 kg [27,760 lb] for all-disposable instruments. The mixed practice produced 36% of the all-disposable waste mass. Waste reduction is the most consistent environmental benefit of reuse.

Energy Consumption

Single-use devices incur manufacturing, packaging and industrial sterilisation energy at every use. Reusable devices incur manufacturing energy once and add washing, thermal disinfection, steam sterilisation and drying energy at each use. The balance depends on reuse count, reprocessing efficiency and the energy intensity of manufacture.

Steam steriliser energy is largely fixed per cycle. Logged data from an Australian hospital steam steriliser over 304 days and 2,173 active cycles gave an electricity demand of approximately 15.7 kWh per cycle plus 0.14 kWh per kg [0.064 kWh per lb] of load. Specific consumption fell from 3 kWh/kg [1.4 kWh/lb] at 5 kg [11 lb] loads to 0.5 kWh/kg [0.23 kWh/lb] at 40 kg [88 lb] loads. Standby accounted for 40% of total electricity. Across 1,343 standard 134 deg C [273 deg F] cycles, the mean load was 21.2 kg [46.7 lb] and 32% of loads were less than 15 kg [33 lb].

Load factor determines whether reuse saves energy. An LCA of dental burs found that reusable burs had 40% lower impact than single-use burs when ultrasonic cleaners and autoclaves operated at optimal capacity. At approximately two-thirds of capacity, four of nine impact categories favoured single-use burs. At approximately one-third of capacity, reusable burs had higher impact in eight of nine categories.

Sub-meter electricity, steam and water on each steriliser and washer-disinfector and track consumption per kg [per lb] processed. Consolidate light loads to rated capacity, schedule cycles around full loads, and rotate idle sterilisers off line instead of holding them on standby, allowing for warm-up and daily air removal test requirements. Centralised departments with continuous throughput achieve higher load factors than benchtop units processing small batches.

Water Usage

Reusable instruments consume water at the facility for washing, rinsing, thermal disinfection, steam generation, vacuum pump sealing and drain tempering. Single-use devices consume water only upstream, in manufacture. Water is the category in which reuse most often performs worse. In the systematic review, mean water use increased when switching to reusable invasive devices, while studies of non-invasive devices reported water reductions of 61 to 70%.

An Australian LCA of anaesthetic equipment for a six-theatre hospital found that conversion to reusables increased annual water use from 34,400 L [9,090 US gal] to 90,600 L [23,930 US gal]. In the Queensland oral health analysis, on-site reprocessing water was 800,800 L [211,500 US gal] per year for current practice and 2,173,600 L [574,200 US gal] for an all-reusable scenario. That analysis counted on-site water only, so the all-disposable scenario recorded zero.

Steriliser water use is highly sensitive to load size. In the logged hospital steriliser, specific water use fell from 200 L/kg [24 US gal/lb] at 5 kg [11 lb] loads to 20 L/kg [2.4 US gal/lb] at 40 kg [88 lb] loads, and standby accounted for 21% of total water. One hospital in the United States reduced water use by approximately 16,300 L [4,300 US gal] per day by replacing water-ejector vacuum systems on four steam sterilisers with electric vacuum pumps and returning jacket condensate to the boiler plant, with an estimated payback of 1.9 years.

Specify mechanical vacuum pumps in place of water ejectors on new or refurbished sterilisers. Supply liquid ring pump seal water from a recirculating chilled water loop instead of single-pass potable water, return jacket condensate where the steam plant allows, and run sterilisers and washer-disinfectors at full load. Final rinse water and steam generator feedwater must continue to meet AS 5369:2023 water quality requirements. In water-constrained regional facilities, include reprocessing water demand in instrument selection. For low-value items with high reprocessing water demand, single-use devices can be the lower-impact option.

Greenhouse Gas Emissions

GHG emissions, expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), integrate manufacture, transport, reprocessing energy and waste treatment. The systematic review reported mean reductions in global warming potential of 38 to 50% when switching to reusables, depending on product group. For invasive devices the mean reduction was 47%, with individual results ranging from a 99% reduction to a 227% increase. The spread reflects reuse count, reprocessing efficiency and the electricity emission factor.

Device-level studies show the range of outcomes. For laparoscopic cholecystectomy in Sweden, a single-use trocar system produced 565 kg [1,246 lb] CO2e per 500 procedures against 118 kg [260 lb] for a reusable system. In the United Kingdom, hybrid laparoscopic instruments (reusable instruments with single-use components) produced 1.76 kg [3.9 lb] CO2e per operation against 7.19 kg [15.9 lb] for single-use equivalents, a 76% reduction at less than half the cost. In the United States, single-use laryngoscope handles produced 16 to 25 times, and single-use blades 6 to 8 times, the emissions of reusable alternatives. In the United Kingdom, repairing reusable surgical scissors reduced the carbon footprint per use by 20% and the life cycle cost per use by 32%.

The electricity emission factor governs reprocessing emissions. An Australian LCA of anaesthetic equipment found that conversion to reusables increased annual emissions by 9%, from 5,095 kg [11,230 lb] to 5,575 kg [12,290 lb] CO2e, on the predominantly coal-fired Australian grid of the time. Under United Kingdom and European grid mixes the same conversion reduced emissions by 84%, and under the United States mix by 48%. It also reduced equipment cost by 46%, approximately AUD 32,000 per year. Calculate reprocessing emissions with the current National Greenhouse Accounts Factors for the relevant state grid, or with the contracted renewable fraction where a renewable power purchase agreement applies. Decarbonising the electricity supply reduces the per-use emissions of reusables and has no effect on the embodied emissions of single-use devices.

Reuse count is the second governing variable. The embodied emissions of a reusable instrument are recovered only after a sufficient number of uses. Premature retirement through damage, loss or set rationalisation raises the per-use footprint. Record the service life of each instrument against the reuse count assumed in the procurement analysis, and repair instead of replacing where a validated repair service is available.

Differences Across Healthcare Settings: Large Hospitals vs. Small and Regional Facilities

Large metropolitan hospitals operate central sterile services departments (CSSDs) with large steam sterilisers, having a chamber volume of 60 L [2.1 cu ft] or greater, automated washer-disinfectors and continuous throughput. High load factors minimise energy and water per instrument, instrument sets accumulate high reuse counts, and the department carries the staffing, validation and traceability systems that AS 5369:2023 requires. These sites are best placed to realise the benefits of reuse.

Small hospitals, regional clinics and office-based practices typically operate small steam sterilisers, with a chamber volume not exceeding 60 L [2.1 cu ft], at low and irregular throughput. Because per-cycle consumption is largely fixed, light loads raise energy and water per instrument, and the fixed cost of validation, maintenance, traceability and competency training is spread across few procedures. Single-use instruments remove this infrastructure and labour, at the cost of higher waste mass, repeat purchasing and transport of clinical waste from remote sites. Where electricity is supplied by diesel generation or water is trucked in, the emissions and water cost of on-site reprocessing per instrument increase further.

Centralised reprocessing is the intermediate option. In the Queensland oral health service, the central department served satellite clinics 3 to 72 km [2 to 45 mi] away by daily courier. Its mixed strategy used 37% of the on-site water and energy of an all-reusable strategy and produced 36% of the waste of an all-disposable strategy. Annual cost was AUD 1.28 million for current practice, AUD 1.23 million for all-reusable instruments and AUD 2.24 million for all-disposable instruments, 75% above current practice. For any hub model, size the instrument set inventory to cover the courier turnaround, transport used instruments in closed, cleanable containers under a documented transport procedure, and include courier emissions in the assessment.

Base each facility's instrument strategy on procedure volume, achievable load factor, access to a compliant central service, local electricity emission factor, water supply, staff competency and cost. Apply the decision item by item. Retain reusables for high-frequency and high-value instruments, and use single-use devices where reprocessing volume does not justify validated capacity.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Reusable instruments reduce solid waste in almost all comparisons and reduce GHG emissions in most, provided reprocessing runs at high load factors on a low-emission electricity supply and instruments reach their design reuse count. Single-use instruments use less water at the facility and avoid reprocessing infrastructure, at the cost of higher waste mass and repeat manufacturing impact. Compliance with AS 5369:2023 and NSQHS Action 3.17 is a fixed constraint on either strategy.

In large hospitals and CSSDs, standardise on reusable instruments where clinically appropriate. Sub-meter each steriliser and washer-disinfector and track kWh and litres per kg [US gal per lb] processed. Consolidate light loads, rotate idle sterilisers off line, specify mechanical vacuum pumps and recirculating seal water on new or refurbished sterilisers, implement instrument repair programmes and procure low-emission electricity.

In small and regional facilities, assess access to a compliant central reprocessing service before investing in on-site capacity. Where on-site reprocessing is retained, size the steriliser to the measured load profile and run cycles at full load. Where single-use devices are used, segregate uncontaminated packaging from clinical waste. Extend the RACGP cost-benefit framework, which covers unit cost, waste removal, sterilisation packaging, steriliser costs, labour, power, water and maintenance, with waste mass, water volume and CO2e per use, and apply it item by item.

For health networks and procurement, require suppliers to state validated reprocessing instructions and expected service life for reusable devices, and take-back or recycling arrangements for single-use devices. Fund centralised reprocessing capacity for regional clinic networks where volumes do not justify validated on-site capacity.
