Introduction

This white paper sets out how to specify, implement and justify remote monitoring of steam sterilisers and washer-disinfectors (WDs) across multi-site Australian hospital networks, using controller data with optional additional sensors. Remote monitoring reduces unplanned downtime by converting detectable degradation into planned work and by identifying the probable cause and parts before site attendance. It does not replace the routine monitoring, cycle record review and release procedures of AS 5369:2023, which superseded AS/NZS 4187:2014 and AS/NZS 4815:2006 in December 2023. The guidance is manufacturer-agnostic and applies to new and retrofitted equipment.

Impact of Unplanned Downtime

An unplanned outage removes reprocessing capacity until the unit is repaired, tested and released back into use. Where the remaining units absorb the load, the cost is mainly overtime, extended turnaround and inter-site transport. Where the unit is a single point of failure, such as the only steriliser at a satellite site, theatre sessions are delayed or cancelled and instrument sets are reprocessed elsewhere or replaced with loan sets.

Quantify downtime from site records rather than published averages, because theatre costs vary with costing method, case mix and jurisdiction. AS 5369:2023 maintenance records already state the reason for and date of each repair, the work done, the parts replaced and the person releasing the equipment back into use. Add time stamps for fault report, site attendance and return to use where they are missing, so that outage duration can be measured.

Do not plan to cover outages with immediate-use sterilisation. AS 5369:2023 prohibits its routine use as a convenience where insufficient devices are available or as a cost-saving mechanism, and requires a business continuity plan with documented backup procedures. Mitigate downtime through installed capacity, inter-site reprocessing arrangements and maintenance performance.

Monitored Parameters and Failure Indicators

Progressive failures of vacuum systems, steam generators, door seals, valves, pumps, dosing systems and water treatment produce measurable degradation before the unit faults. Sudden failures of controllers, power supplies and electronics generally give no usable precursor. Remote monitoring reduces downtime only for progressive failures, so classify each unit's failure history by failure mode before setting expectations.

For steam sterilisers, record chamber temperature at the reference measurement point and chamber pressure on every cycle at the resolution provided by the controller data interface. During the holding period, AS 5369:2023 requires the measured temperature and the saturated steam temperature calculated from chamber pressure to remain within the sterilisation temperature band, e.g. 134 to 137 deg C [273 to 279 deg F] under EN 285 for a 134 deg C [273 deg F] process with a 3 min holding time. Trend the difference between the two values on every cycle. A measured temperature higher than the calculated value indicates superheat. A lower measured temperature indicates air or non-condensable gases. Both develop as trends before a cycle fault or a failed Bowie and Dick-type test.

On-board instrumentation does not measure steam quality. AS 5369:2023 requires steam quality tests for large sterilisers at installation, relocation or a change to the steam supply, with acceptance criteria of a dryness value of at least 0.95, non-condensable gases less than 3.5% v/v and superheat not exceeding 25 deg C [45 deg F]. Use the temperature difference trend, air detector output and steam generator data to decide when an additional test is required.

Record every automated vacuum leak rate test result. The EN 285 limit is a pressure rise of 0.13 kPa/min [0.019 psi/min]. Set a condition alert below the limit, e.g. 0.09 kPa/min [0.013 psi/min], so that door seal or valve leakage is repaired at a planned time rather than after a failed test.

For WDs, record disinfection stage temperature and time on every cycle and calculate A0. AS 5369:2023 lists 10 min at 80 deg C [176 deg F] and 1 min at 90 deg C [194 deg F] as common holding times for A0 600. Trend the margin above the validated minimum, not only pass or fail. Where the controller reports dosed volume and final rinse conductivity, trending them detects faults between the quarterly dosing volumetric test and the default monthly conductivity test required by AS 5369:2023.

System Architecture and Cyber Security

Read cycle, alarm and test data in read-only mode from the controller's existing data interface, such as a serial printer output, Modbus TCP or OPC UA. Basic machine-connected monitoring uses this data only. Additional sensors on the machine or on water treatment, steam and compressed air services are a separate scope at additional cost. Add sensors only where the controller lacks the signal, and use non-invasive devices such as clamp-on current transformers, surface-mounted temperature sensors and pump vibration sensors. A sensor that penetrates the chamber, pipework or control circuit is an equipment change. Assess it under AS 5369:2023 and repeat the affected qualification stages where process effectiveness could be altered. Install retrofit wiring to AS/NZS 3000 using a licensed electrician.

Connect each unit through a connection device on the machine, or through a central gateway serving several machines, that buffers data for the longest credible network outage. Place equipment and connection devices on a segregated network segment that permits only outbound encrypted connections (TLS 1.2 or later) to the platform, with no inbound ports open. Where hospital ICT policy excludes equipment from the clinical network, use 4G, either a modem on each machine or a central 4G gateway where every machine is within reach of its short-range wireless signal. Confirm 4G signal strength in the plant room and wireless reach to each machine at site survey. Synchronise device clocks to a network time source so that cycle records align with the instrument tracking system.

Require multi-factor authentication and role-based access, and restrict remote access to viewing data. Do not permit remote changes to cycle parameters or controller software. AS 5369:2023 requires changes to controlling or monitoring software that affect equipment operation to be checked, and a remote change bypasses on-site change assessment. Define in the service agreement who maintains gateway firmware and platform software and how updates are tested.

Include the monitoring network in the hospital's medical device cyber security risk register, applying the TGA guidance Medical device cyber security information for users. Under the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018, critical hospitals (those with a general intensive care unit) must report cyber incidents, and designated hospitals must maintain a critical infrastructure risk management program. Cycle records can contain operator identifiers and tracking links, so define the transmitted data fields, exclude patient identifiers and confirm the hosting location against the health service's data residency policy.

Alert Design and Maintenance Planning

Establish a baseline for each trended parameter from at least 30 production cycles after performance qualification or requalification, separated by cycle type. Set condition alerts at a defined fraction of the margin between baseline and validated limit. Forward controller process alarms to the platform for notification, but do not use the platform as the primary means of fault detection. Assign each alert type a response and response window, e.g. inspection within 5 working days. Review alerts quarterly and remove or retune any alert without a defined action or with a high false alarm rate.

Configure alerts for missed routine tests. AS 5369:2023 requires a daily Bowie and Dick-type test on sterilisers that use vacuum air removal, a weekly leak rate test and air detector function test where an air detector is fitted, and a daily leak rate test before the Bowie and Dick-type test where none is fitted. Alert when no conforming test cycle precedes the first production cycle of the day.

AS 5369:2023 states that preventive maintenance and recalibration frequency should be based on maintenance and calibration history, with a maximum of one year between recalibrations, and requires a maximum permitted delay in scheduled maintenance to be specified. Use failure and trend data to set and justify each unit's interval and task content, and track maintenance delay on the platform.

Combine condition-based work orders with scheduled visits by geographic cluster, and schedule planned outages in low-demand periods agreed with theatre management. Hold spares centrally for parts with high fleet failure frequency, and at regional sites for parts whose transport time exceeds the acceptable outage. Compare identical models across sites and investigate outliers in cycle time, pump-down time and failure rate for installation, services or usage differences.

Before dispatch, review alarm history, cycle traces and condition data to identify the probable cause and parts. Operating errors such as incorrect cycle selection or a closed supply valve can be corrected by telephone guidance. AS 5369:2023 requires equipment with a fault that could affect device quality or operator safety to be removed from use immediately. Site staff confirm that the unit is non-operational and tag it out of service pending attendance. The unit is not returned to use until maintenance is completed and documented with the releasing person named, so remote diagnosis still requires an on-site documented return to use.

Records, Release and Change Control

AS 5369:2023 permits paper or electronic process records. Electronic records require procedures that confirm attainment of process parameters at the end of every cycle, and the standard notes that facilities should be working towards electronic tracking and process records. A monitoring platform record is a secondary record for trending, alerting and retrieval. Release loads from the controller record unless the platform has been validated as the release record for data completeness, time stamp accuracy and agreement with the controller record, and is controlled under the facility's record procedures.

Requalification is required at least annually and after major changes or repairs that could affect process efficacy, with the data compared against the original qualification. Platform data does not replace thermometric testing, performance qualification or requalification by a competent person using calibrated test instruments. Use it to identify drift between requalifications and to select units for investigation. Instrumentation that controls or monitors the process must be calibrated at specified intervals with traceability to national or international standards. Label retrofit condition sensors as non-process instruments so that they are not used for release decisions.

Where monitoring, maintenance or testing is outsourced, AS 5369:2023 requires an agreement defining each party's responsibilities. Specify alert response times, alert ownership, data ownership, export format at contract end, cyber security responsibilities and access revocation. Advisory AS24/01 under NSQHS Standards Action 3.17 required a gap analysis against AS 5369:2023 by 30 June 2025 and an implemented compliance plan, and requires the economically useful life of reprocessing equipment to be recorded in the asset register. Set and review that life from failure rate and maintenance cost per cycle rather than a fixed age.

Cost Model and Return on Investment

Build the investment case from the hospital's own maintenance records and costing data. The model has six parts: baseline failure and outage data, consequence cost per outage hour, downtime benefit mechanisms, additional benefits and exclusions, system cost, and evaluation with sensitivity analysis. The worked example applies the model to a four-site network of 10 steam sterilisers and 14 WDs. Inputs in the example are assumptions unless a source is stated. Replace each one with site data, quoted prices and the organisation's financial parameters before use.

Baseline Failure and Outage Data

Extract at least three years of corrective work orders for each unit. Count unplanned failures per unit per year by equipment type, excluding planned preventive maintenance, and measure each outage from fault report to documented return to use. Split each outage into five components: response from fault report to site attendance, diagnosis, parts wait, repair, and testing and release. Parts wait is the fraction of events that need a second attendance multiplied by the mean wait for parts and that attendance. Record building services failures separately, because basic machine-connected monitoring does not monitor services.

The example assumes 2.5 failures per steriliser per year and 1.5 failures per WD per year, a diagnosis time of 1.5 h, a repair time of 2 h, a testing and release time of 1.5 h, and a second attendance on 25% of events. Response time, parts wait and attendance cost depend on location. Unplanned attendance cost includes labour, travel and after-hours loading, and a second attendance costs the same again.

Downtime Benefit Mechanisms

Monitoring removes unplanned outage hours by three mechanisms. Calculate each separately so that the pilot can verify each one. The first is conversion of failures to planned work. The fraction converted, p, equals the progressive failure fraction multiplied by detection effectiveness. Obtain the progressive fraction by classifying historical failures by failure mode and counting those with a precursor visible in controller cycle, alarm and test data. Detection effectiveness is the fraction of progressive failures detected with enough lead time to plan the repair and acted on before failure. Each converted failure is replaced by a 4 h planned intervention in a low-demand period, costed at the Class 1 rate, with parts ordered in advance and an attendance combined with a scheduled visit at A$400.

The second mechanism is remote resolution. The fraction r of the remaining failures comprises operating errors and resettable faults diagnosed from platform data and corrected by telephone guidance. Each takes 2 h from fault report to documented return to use and avoids one unplanned attendance. The third mechanism is shorter on-site repair. Automatic fault notification shortens response, review of cycle traces and alarm history before dispatch shortens diagnosis, and identifying parts before attendance reduces the fraction of events needing a second attendance. Repair and testing times are unchanged.

Additional Benefits and Exclusions

Planned replacement of a degrading component avoids secondary damage from running to failure, such as pump damage after bearing failure. The example assumes a parts saving of A$300 per converted failure, giving A$1,700 per year in the low case and A$2,900 in the moderate case. Derive the site value by comparing the parts cost of planned and unplanned repairs of the same failure mode in work orders.

Exclude benefits that the monitoring system does not deliver or that cannot be costed from site data. Basic machine-connected monitoring does not monitor water treatment, steam supply or compressed air, so failures of these services are excluded. Cost additional sensors for these services as a separate scope with their own benefits. Monitoring does not switch idle units off, so energy and water savings from a switch-off schedule are not attributable to it. The platform record does not replace the controller cycle record or validation by a competent person, so record handling and validation costs are unchanged. Infection risk reduction, extended equipment life, and avoided recalls or deferred capital purchases without supporting records are also excluded. Where site records show recall events, or utilisation data shows that a planned steriliser purchase can be deferred, cost them separately.

For a single machine, the hospital network subscription of A$240 per year is less than the A$600 cost of one Class 3 outage hour and one fifth of one metropolitan unplanned attendance at A$1,200.

Implementation in Multi-Site Networks

Run a 6 to 12 month pilot on the highest-consequence units, covering each equipment model in the fleet. Define measures before go-live: unplanned outage hours per unit, mean time from fault report to return to use, detection effectiveness, share of calls closed without attendance, first-time fix rate, preventive maintenance compliance and missed routine tests. Compare against the baseline and recalculate the investment case with the measured values before extending deployment.

Where the service provider triages alerts, nominate a contact at each site to confirm non-operational status and tag the unit out of service, and a central contact with authority to approve planned outages across sites. Escalate to the site reprocessing manager when a unit must be removed from use. Include alert response in the staff training and competency records required by AS 5369:2023.

Conclusion

Remote monitoring reduces sterilisation equipment downtime where failures have measurable precursors and where diagnosis and parts availability determine repair duration. Downtime value is highest at units without capacity redundancy and at sites with long service travel times. At metropolitan sites with capacity redundancy, the value comes from fewer attendances and shorter repairs rather than avoided theatre impact. Base the investment case on failure-mode classification, consequence costing and break-even analysis from site records, and confirm detection effectiveness in a pilot before network-wide deployment. Use read-only data paths, segregated networks and outbound-only connections, and keep load release, change assessment and return to use under on-site AS 5369:2023 procedures.
